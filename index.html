I am a computer science graduate from LUMS, where I am advised by Professor Fareed Zaffar and Dr Ashish Gehani.

My research focuses on the security of autonomous AI agents, mostly through measurement. I study how agents are attacked and misused in the wild, whether published claims about AI systems hold up when someone else reruns them, and how to tell a compromised or adversarial agent apart from its behavior alone, since what an agent says about itself is not evidence.

I am currently working with Dr. Ashish Gehani, Dr. Ramneet Kaur and Eric Yeh at SRI International and with Professor Yasir Zaki at NYU Abu Dhabi.

I am applying to PhD and research-based master’s programs this cycle for Fall 2027 entry.

Publications

  1. Decomposing Error in Continual World Models M. S. Hassan*, M. M. Irfan*, M. F. Zaffar  (*equal contribution) NeurIPS 2026 Workshop pdf·code
    SummaryContinual world models are usually judged by how accurate they are after each update, but that does not tell you whether the update itself tracked what actually changed in the environment. We split endpoint error into the error a model inherited from before and the mismatch between how the model changed and how the environment changed. Endpoint error can favor throwing the old model away over faithfully revising it, while mismatch alone rewards keeping old bias. In controlled linear experiments the ranking of update rules flips on all 544 usable trials, and a learned neural model on a nonlinear simulator shows the same effect, shrinking as inherited error falls. We propose reporting all three errors together as the evaluation protocol.
  2. Understanding or Imitation? Auditing Conceptual Understanding and Reasoning in Large Language Models M. S. Hassan, E. Jan, R. Kaur, E. Yeh, M. F. Zaffar, A. Gehani ACM Conference on Reproducibility and Replicability (REP), 2026 pdf·code
    SummaryThe "Potemkin Understanding in LLMs" paper (ICML 2025) argues that benchmarks credit LLMs with conceptual understanding they do not possess. We independently reproduced both of its evaluation techniques and extended them to smaller and reasoning models. The directional claims stand, but the reported scores are unstable, varying by up to 51% across identical runs, and the original setup lets a model grade its own answers: switching to a cross-model judge shifts the headline rate by 0.11. Reasoning models cut the incoherence by an order of magnitude, which points to the inference regime rather than the model. The point is that a single, self-graded run is not enough evidence for a claim about LLMs.
  3. Data Doping or True Intelligence? Evaluating the Transferability of Injected Knowledge in LLMs E. Jan, M. Ali, M. S. Hassan, M. F. Zaffar, Y. Zaki Findings of EMNLP, 2025 pdf·acl anthology·arxiv
    SummaryWe injected new knowledge into five model families and tested whether the models could genuinely use it beyond the task they were finetuned on or only recite it as it was given. We found that comprehension-style tasks led to substantially greater knowledge transfer than surface-level mappings such as translation.
    In a nutshell, how you teach a model matters as much as what you teach it.
  1. SoK: Systematizing Prompt Injection Against Autonomous LLM Agents in preparation · targeting IEEE S&P 2027
    SummaryAn agent that browses the web will read whatever the web puts in front of it, and some of that text is written specifically to hijack it. Across 84 papers I am mapping these attacks by where the adversarial text enters the agent loop (user prompt, web content, memory, tool outputs, hidden HTML) and testing which defenses actually hold. One early finding: a model’s resistance to attacks in the user prompt says little about attacks arriving through tools or memory, so jailbreak-style evaluations do not transfer to agents.
  2. Listeners Know More Than They Say: Probing White-Box Listeners to Flag Black-Box Senders for LLM Multi-Agent Systems submitted to NAACL
    SummaryAgents from different owners are starting to interact in open systems, and some of them will lie. In a team that mixes open-weight (white-box) and closed (black-box) agents, a linear probe on an honest listener’s hidden state registers a false claim even when the listener never voices it, provided the listener holds the contradicted fact (8 models, 1.7B to 70B). Pooled over three listeners in six-agent teams, the probes remove an instructed liar in 39% of games, against 2% when the listeners are simply asked, with about 5% wrongful removals. The theory says why: with no witness a liar is undetectable, and misses and false removals decay exponentially in the number of witnesses. Adaptive attackers evade by avoiding witnesses, not by fooling the probe.
  3. Escalate, Don’t Improvise: Comparability in Autonomous Measurement Fleets (AmiClaw) in preparation · targeting HotMobile 2027
    SummaryMeasurement studies are still run by hand: a researcher designs the experiment, babysits the runs, and stitches the data together. I am building fleets of autonomous agents that run these studies themselves. The catch is that improvising agents could quietly produce incomparable results so I argue an agent that hits ambiguity should escalate it to a fixed protocol instead of improvising around it on its own.
